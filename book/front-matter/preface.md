# Preface

In 2020, shortly after the pandemic began, my coworker Melissa and I were lamenting that we couldn't give people tours of our factory anymore.
Every time we brought an engineer into the factory, their eyes lit up, and they could not stop asking questions.
They could finally see all of the impacts their decisions had in the effort to bring their designs to life.
We missed this experience so much that we knew we had to do something about it.

The Pick, Place, Podcast was born in June of 2020.
From the start, the goal was to share what it takes to bring a circuit board into the world.
We wanted to try to bring that same feeling, of stepping into the factory, into listeners' ears.

"Why does it matter if I bury my polarity indicators in the middle of the part?
Don't machines build the circuit boards anyway?"

"Who cares if solder bridges these two leads together?
They're already connected by copper."

There were all of these small little bits of friction that, added together, created a circuit board that was, at best, difficult to build and, at worst, nearly impossible to build more than a handful of.

I started listening to podcasts back in 2005 when Leo Laporte first launched This Week in Tech.
I loved the medium for passively acquiring knowledge while performing otherwise mundane tasks.
I knew it would be a perfect solution to sharing all of this knowledge I had bottled up in my brain.

I had tried many times to write this information down, but found the view was not worth the climb.
The amount of time it took me to draft and edit and publish the material was far more than I had available.
But with a podcast, the density of information was far greater, and a podcast took significantly less effort than blog posts.

For years we've been asked, "When are you going to write a book?" and "Why no video podcast?" (keep waiting).
Frankly, it's because it's just too difficult and too time consuming.
The density of knowledge from podcasting was tough to compete with.

But now we find ourselves in 2026.
AI has completely changed the world, whether people are aware of it yet or not.
And two very important things converged that made this book possible.

1. The remarkably accessible tools for creating your own custom PCBs, even with almost no knowledge about what PCBs are or how they work.
2. The remarkable ability for LLMs to transcribe audio, digest vast quantities of information, and distill it down to high density knowledge.

It is my belief that PCB design as we know it today, November of 2026, will be a thing of the past, practiced only by people who love to do it, much like painters still love to create photorealistic artwork, even after decades of photographic technology becoming ubiquitous.
AI will commoditize the design of circuit boards, allowing anybody who needs one to make one, and judging by the initial designs we are seeing from AI that go through our factory, people need help.

Over one million words have been transcribed from the podcast's 92 episodes.
That's nearly twice the length of War and Peace and almost five times the length of Moby Dick.

From the very beginning, it was important to us that the podcast remain free.
And while we mention our employers, we do our very best not to turn the show into an ad for them.
The knowledge is, and always has been, the core value we wanted to generate.

This book is no different.
While both companies are occasionally mentioned, it was important to me that it not come across as an ad for either company.
The goal was to dispense this knowledge in a way that was as easy to find and learn from as possible.

I fully appreciate that my experience is unique, and my understanding of what it takes to get a circuit board into the world is limited.
So I knew that the book had to be open source.
It needs a community of people working on it, correcting it, and improving it.
Without that, it would be limited to just a snapshot of one engineer's experience, in one factory.

And thus, OpenDFX was born.

People need valuable information to improve their designs.
Without it, the AI slop people see today when scrolling their social media feeds will be closely reflected on manufacturing floors worldwide.

While AI was used to generate the vast majority of the text of this book (except for this opening preamble), every word of it was read, edited, and approved by me, including each drawing and table of information.

I hope you find this information valuable, and I hope you create something amazing, and make a dent in the universe.

Made with love in Westfield, Massachusetts.\
\- Chris Denney
